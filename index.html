1. Introduction

This e-Portfolio documents my academic and professional development across twelve units of the Machine Learning module. It includes practical programming exercises, seminars, collaborative discussions, group assessment and individual research. My learning progressed from exploratory data analysis and regression to clustering, neural networks, explainable AI and ethical evaluation.

The evidence addresses legal, social, ethical and professional responsibilities (LO1); dataset suitability and limitations (LO2); critical application of machine-learning methods under uncertainty (LO3); and effective virtual teamwork (LO4).

2. Units 1–4: Exploratory Data Analysis and Regression

Unit 2 explored missing values, skewness, kurtosis, correlations, scatter plots and categorical variables in the Auto-MPG dataset. Six missing horsepower values were identified. MPG correlated negatively with vehicle weight (r = −0.83), displacement (r = −0.80) and cylinders (r = −0.78). Correlation supported feature exploration but did not establish causation.

Unit 3 introduced covariance, Pearson correlation, simple and multiple linear regression, and polynomial regression. These exercises demonstrated how observations and model complexity affect fitted relationships.

Unit 4 investigated fuel-consumption regression and the relationship between population and GDP indicators across countries from 2001 to 2021, reinforcing the importance of variable selection and cautious interpretation (LO2–LO3).

3. Units 5–6: Similarity and Clustering

Unit 5 explored Jaccard dissimilarity using categorical attribute–value pairs. Distances were 0.7273 (Jack–Mary), 0.4444 (Jack–Jim) and 0.9231 (Jim–Mary). The representation of categorical attributes affects the resulting similarity measures.

The K-Means wiki activity illustrated centroid assignment, iteration and sensitivity to initialization. Unit 6 extended this learning through Iris clustering with K = 3. Clusters do not necessarily correspond to known labels, so validation is important (Rousseeuw, 1987).

4. Unit 6: Airbnb Group Project

Group 5Classical ML

The group analysed New York City Airbnb listing prices and supply segmentation using the AB_NYC_2019 dataset (Dgomonov, 2019). Models included Linear Regression, Random Forest (Breiman, 2001) and XGBoost (Chen and Guestrin, 2016), alongside K-Means clustering.

The tuned XGBoost model performed best overall, although prediction errors remained substantial for expensive listings. The four clusters represented high-turnover performers, listed-but-not-booking properties, commercial operators and dormant hosts. Because the data described listings rather than guests, this was supply segmentation, not customer segmentation.

Tutor feedback praised the methodology and visualisations but called for stronger business interpretation, model justification and critical evaluation. Peer assessment highlighted communication, coordination and collective review as professional development priorities (LO2–LO4).

5. Units 7–8: Neural Networks and Optimisation

Unit 7 covered simple perceptrons, AND classification and multilayer networks with sigmoid activation. These exercises demonstrated the role of weights, network structure and activation functions.

Unit 8 investigated gradient descent. At 100 iterations, learning rate 0.08 produced cost 0.004121, while rate 0.01 produced 0.480407. At 500 iterations, rate 0.01 reduced cost to 0.031985. Rate 0.10 led to divergence, showing why step-size selection matters.

The Unit 8 seminar compared conventional ML and deep learning. A MovieLens 100K recommender exercise considered time-aware evaluation, matrix factorisation, RMSE, MAE and explainability (LO2–LO3).

6. Units 9–10: CNN Object Recognition

Unit 9 explored CNN recognition and its ethical implications. In Unit 10, the model correctly classified a car as an automobile with 99.59% confidence, but incorrectly classified a teacup as an airplane with 52.78% confidence. Confidence does not guarantee correctness, especially for unfamiliar inputs.

7. Unit 11: Individual Pneumonia Detection Project

Individual ResearchMedical Imaging

This project compared Histogram of Oriented Gradients with a Linear Support Vector Machine (Cortes and Vapnik, 1995) against a custom Convolutional Neural Network, using chest X-ray images (Mooney, 2018). The protocol included preprocessing, a locked group-aware split, five-fold cross-validation and an independent holdout of 1,165 images.

The results challenge the assumption that a more complex neural network necessarily generalises better. False negatives are especially important in a clinical context.

Grad-CAM sometimes highlighted regions outside the main lung fields, raising concerns about shortcut learning. However, attention maps alone cannot establish the causal basis of a prediction (Selvaraju et al., 2017). Neither model should be used autonomously in clinical practice without external validation, fairness assessment and professional oversight.

10. Final Critical Reflection

Framework: What? So What? Now What? (Rolfe, Freshwater and Jasper, 2001).

What? – My Learning Experience

The module changed my understanding of machine learning from a predominantly technical activity to a process that combines data quality, validation, interpretability and ethical responsibility. Early work on EDA and regression was followed by clustering, neural networks, gradient descent, recommendation systems and CNNs. The Airbnb group assessment and pneumonia individual project provided contrasting business and healthcare settings in which to test these ideas.

So What? – Critical Evaluation

In the Airbnb project, tuned XGBoost outperformed the linear baseline overall, but accuracy differed substantially between price bands. Tutor feedback showed that model results must be translated into business recommendations with clear limitations. The pneumonia project reinforced this lesson in a higher-risk context: HOG–SVM outperformed the custom CNN, including fewer false negatives, demonstrating why error consequences matter as much as aggregate metrics.

Grad-CAM suggested possible attention to non-lung image regions, but did not prove shortcut learning (Selvaraju et al., 2017). The absence of reliable demographic data also prevented a defensible fairness assessment. Collaborative discussions strengthened my understanding of resilience, authorship and accountability. Teamwork highlighted the need for clearer checkpoints and shared responsibility. Gradient descent divergence and the teacup misclassification further demonstrated why unexpected outputs require investigation rather than unquestioning acceptance.

Now What? – Future Professional Development

I will document dataset provenance, preprocessing, data splits, hyperparameters and evaluation procedures to improve reproducibility. I will strengthen calibration, external validation and fairness analysis, particularly for healthcare applications. I also intend to communicate technical uncertainty and practical implications more clearly to non-technical stakeholders. For virtual teamwork, I will introduce regular checkpoints, shared documents and earlier review. The central lesson is that responsible machine learning requires transparent methods, critical evaluation and accountability, not merely accurate predictions.
